About

Short version

For three years I ran retreats where founders and executives learned to hear each other. Fourteen of them, plus one-on-one work, over three hundred people trained in nonviolent communication. Then I spent four years and $165,000 trying to put that into software. The retreats earned about five times what the software did. I closed the software company in May 2026.

That is most of what I know about the difference between work that pays and work that should pay.

Decato

I build it with Vladimir Borisov. You hand it a floor plan and a brief. It lays out a room, sources products that exist and fit, and assembles something a client can approve and a supplier can quote.

The picture was never the hard part. Anyone can generate a beautiful room now, free, in about thirty seconds, and architects say so openly. The hard part is that the sofa in the picture has to be a sofa you can buy, at a price, that fits through the door.

When we went looking for what designers actually complain about, the most useful finding was inverted. Prettier drawings cost them money: put a polished render on the cover sheet and the subcontractors bid higher. Some practices deliberately show less. So the thing to sell is not beauty and not speed. It is the check at the moment someone picks a product.

What Empy taught me

Empy began as a Slack bot that noticed when a team was fighting and became real-time coaching for high-stakes calls. 43 companies came through pilots and subscriptions. We raised $165,000, ran US pilots with Erickson Coaching International and Facility Solutions Group, and came second on Product Hunt.

It failed anyway, and I can account for it. I was driven by a mission instead of a problem. A mission lives in the future. A problem lives now. I was not building because somebody suffered every day and would pay to stop; I was building because I believed the world ought to work a certain way.

What makes that hard to catch is that a mission excuses the absence of feedback. No money means you are not in it for the money. No users means you are not chasing the mass market. No growth means you are laying a foundation. Each explanation is more intelligent than the last, and you can go years without noticing that none of them is a customer.

The through-line

I only saw the pattern recently. As a kid I built Warcraft III custom maps: you take an engine someone else wrote, put your own rules inside it, and let other people live in there. At eighteen I ran a small web agency while I was still at school. While I was at Google I finished a master's in psychoanalysis, and a year later I was training a model to tell an evaluation apart from an observation in human speech. I have practised Ashtanga since 2019 and taught it, the same sequence in the same order for years.

Maps, conversations, rooms. Each time the job is the same. Take a situation people keep getting wrong, find the rules underneath it, build something that holds them.

It also explains a failure mode I have. I am drawn to the systems and bored by the shipping, which is how you end up with four years of intelligent work and no revenue.

Before I risked my own money

Ten years on other people's. Google from 2020 to 2021, five enterprise accounts including Semrush, Miro, Veeam and Wrike, market entry models in SQL and Python. Before that Cryptology in Singapore, where I hired seven people and watched daily trading volume go from $100K to $2.8M. Artec 3D across six countries. adidas and Reebok on budgets up to $3.9M a quarter.

None of it taught me how to build a company. All of it taught me what a number has to do before it is worth putting in a sentence.

Now

Decato, from Yerevan. I spent 2025 advising Pink Elephant, an AI and GTM fund, on deal evaluation and positioning, and I work with Armenian Code Academy on how founders actually adopt AI rather than how they say they do.
