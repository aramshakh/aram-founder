Projects

Seven things I have built since I was twenty-one. Three made money, one is running now, the rest are closed. Each one is here with what actually happened to it, because a list of launches without endings tells you nothing.

2026 to nowDecato

Interior design workflow software, built with Vladimir Borisov. You hand it a floor plan and a brief. It lays out an editable room, sources products that exist and fit, and assembles something a client can approve and a supplier can quote.

Fifty people have used it. The average first session runs about six hours, which is the number I watch most, and the first paying customer is in the US. Small numbers, but real ones.

It is narrower than anything I built before, on purpose. Empy failed because I chose a mission over a problem, so this time the wedge is a single check somebody performs every day: does this product actually fit this room, at this price.

2026 to nowHermes

Hermes Agent is an open-source framework for personal agents. I did not write it, I deployed it on a VPS I rent and then built my operations on top of it.

It runs twenty-two scheduled jobs. Nine are Decato: search console pulls, a watchdog that diffs which pages are actually indexed, technical checks, a content backlog, a weekly operations report, lead digests, conversion alerts. Others draft the weekly investor update, sweep for follow-ups that have gone stale, and run a finance check-in every evening. Two keep Hermes itself alive: a nightly private backup, and a weekly self-update that refuses to run if the repository is dirty.

I talk to it in Telegram, one thread per subject, and it keeps notes across months rather than across a session.

In 2026 I ran workshops in Yerevan where thirty people brought a laptop and had their own agent running by the end of the afternoon. Five mentors volunteered, unpaid.

It is the least impressive thing on this page and the one I use most. Everything else here was built to be sold; this one was assembled because I needed it, which is probably the lesson.

2021 to 2026Empy.ai

It started as research: a transformer fine-tuned to tell an evaluation apart from an observation in written speech, at 82% F1. Then a Slack bot that caught conflict in team chat. Then a macOS app that sat in a call and coached you through it in real time.

Four thousand users. A $165,000 pre-seed. 43 companies through pilots and subscriptions. Second product of the day on Product Hunt. A Burning Heroes final in San Francisco and a live pitch at Plug and Play Silicon Valley. US pilots with Erickson Coaching International and Facility Solutions Group. Draper University, then Plug and Play Armenia.

The Electron build crashed often enough to lose every user it gained, so in February 2026 I locked myself in a room for three weeks and rewrote it in Swift. That fixed the crashes and did not fix the business. I closed it in May 2026.

What it taught me is on the about page and at more length in this post: I was driven by a mission rather than a problem, and a mission excuses the absence of feedback for years at a time.

2022A housing bot for people leaving

In 2022 a great many people arrived in Armenia at once and could not find anywhere to live. I built a Telegram bot that pulled listings and matched them to what people were asking for, and a channel around it.

I built it and sold it inside three weeks. The channel is still running, with over six thousand members.

It is the fastest thing I have ever shipped and the only one I sold. Not because the idea was clever, but because the problem was screaming and I was standing next to it.

2020 to 2025Retreat Zone

It started with running them myself. Four days in a house in the mountains for tech managers who were burning out: nonviolent communication workshops, Ashtanga in the morning, ecstatic dance, a tea ceremony, and a lot of unstructured time. $1,700 a head.

Fourteen retreats, plus one-on-one work, and over three hundred people trained in nonviolent communication. In one six-month stretch it did $70,000 in revenue. Across the same years it earned roughly five times what the startup did.

Retreat Zone was the attempt to turn that into a business rather than a calendar: an aggregator, because the people who most need to disconnect are the worst at booking it. I wanted to help someone pick their first retreat, check it actually fit what they needed, and handle the transfers and accommodation around it. I raised my first investor check on it. It did not work, and I stopped.

The practice paid. The product built on top of the practice did not. I have made that mistake once more since, with Empy, which is why Decato starts from something someone already pays to have done.
